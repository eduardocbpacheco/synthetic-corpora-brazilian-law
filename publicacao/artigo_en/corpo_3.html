4.1What each pre-training regime yields, and on which task

Compare the no pre-training row with the two that follow it, in the same model and the same column. On Tucano2 3.7B, averaged over the six blocks, discursive questions go from 28.0% in the control to 28.1% with raw text and 29.7% with cards. The formal part of document writing goes from 32.1% to 36.0% with raw text and 34.7% with cards. The merit part goes from 23.2% to 26.2% and 25.5%.

Put differently: both pre-training regimes improve legal document writing, and neither improves discursive questions appreciably. Raw text yields +3.9 points on the formal part and +0.02 on discursive questions, which on discursive questions amounts to nothing. Cards yield +2.6 on the formal part and +1.7 on discursive questions. The pattern repeats on the other three models where a control exists. The effect size depends on the volume of supervision that follows. On block I, with 1,007 examples, raw-text pre-training takes the formal part of Tucano2 3.7B from 27.3% to 36.0%, a gain of 8.8 points. On block VI, with 2,909, it takes it from 39.2% to 41.1%, a gain of 1.9. The pre-training gain shrinks as fine-tuning grows.

The block 0 column shows that this gain is the recovery of a deficit, and not an increment over a tie. Before any task example, pre-training on raw text leaves the model worse than it was: on Tucano2 3.7B the formal part falls from 21.9% to 6.2% and the merit part from 23.3% to 11.0%, and the drop repeats across all four models, averaging 9.4 points on form and 10.1 on merit. Pre-training on cards costs far less, 3.1 and 2.4 points, and is the only one of the two that improves discursive questions at this stage, by 2.8 points on average. This is consistent with the stability gap reported for continual pre-training, an initial drop before the model recovers [19]. A model delivered in the block-0 state with raw text would be worse than the model with no treatment at all; it is fine-tuning that recovers the deficit and, from there, overtakes the control.

4.2The two corpus regimes are not substitutes

Now compare the last two sub-rows of each model. On discursive questions the cards beat raw text in nearly every column of all four models; on the formal part of document writing the reverse holds, and raw text wins in practically all of them. On the two Qwen3 models the two document cuts have opposite signs: cards win on merit and lose on form, and the aggregate document score, being 85.1% merit, records only the merit sign.

The most direct explanation is compositional: a full appellate decision is a procedural document and carries addressing, party identification and closing, whereas a card is an excerpt of a provision accompanied by questions and answers. Each regime teaches what its material contains. The division of labour was quantified at the criterion level on Tucano2 3.7B. The correlation between one regime's gain and the other's, criterion by criterion, is ρ = +0.30 (Spearman; Pearson r = +0.37), closer to zero than to one. Of the criteria improved by at least one regime, 218 are exclusive to one of them against 103 shared. Taking, criterion by criterion, the better of the two rates yields an upper bound for the union that sits 4.92 points above the best single arm on the formal part, 2.55 on merit and 2.21 on discursive questions. None of the 180 conditions was trained on a mixed corpus, so this value is a target and not a result.

This formal ordering is, however, specific to the coverage of the frozen corpus. Section 4.6 reports that completing the statutory coverage of the card arm reverses it on both document cuts, and that the card format in fact gains on document form once its material is present.

4.3Block composition matters more than block size

The blocks do not form a ladder of volume, and this changes what the contrasts between them measure. The contrast I against II is not one of volume: the two blocks are disjoint and of similar size, 1,007 against 1,182, so it compares replacing official Bar material with generated material. The result is weak on every task, with a mean gain of −0.03 points on the formal part and one significant arm out of nine; swapping the official for the generated buys nothing. The contrast II against III adds 499 cascaded synthetic documents and is a genuine addition: null on discursive questions, with two significant arms out of nine and a mean gain of +0.02, and significant in nine out of nine on the formal part, with +4.44 on average. The contrast I against IV adds all the generated material to the official, 1,680 training examples, and has the largest effect, with eight arms out of nine on the formal part and +5.90 mean gain, against one arm out of nine on discursive questions and +0.31. Finally IV against VI, which adds the 223 examples from other career examinations, is null on every task.

None of the four contrasts isolates volume from composition, because each addition brings material of a kind that was not there. What holds is that adding generated material to the official one pays off, and pays off on document writing; that replacing the official with the generated does not; and that the last addition, of material from another genre, pays off nowhere. The spread across blocks says the same: the distance between the best and worst column of each row runs from 4.3 to 6.2 points on discursive questions and from 18.5 to 28.8 on the formal part. What the blocks teach differently is, above all, document form.

Block V is the sharpest case. It holds 223 examples from legal career examinations and no Bar material, and in Tables 2 and 3 it is the worst column of nearly every row, with the deficit concentrated on the formal part. The explanation is genre, and it makes a testable prediction: a judgment has no addressing, because a judge addresses no one, and neither does a legal opinion. Block V lowers the addressing criteria by 19.4 to 26.0 points across all four models, without exception.

4.4Comparison with the base model

The tables give the experiment's internal starting point. It remains to compare them with the model as published, without pre-training and without fine-tuning. On document writing the pipeline works on all four models, and the gain concentrates on the formal cut: from 17.5 to 22.9 percentage points, against 5.6 to 11.8 on merit. On discursive questions it works on the two Tucano2 models, with 3.9 and 6.8 points, and fails on the two Qwen3 models: on the 8B only one of the twelve trained conditions beats the base, and on the 14B none does, the best falling 4.0 points short. The reading is not one of degradation but of reallocation. The whole pipeline teaches the form of the procedural document, and what it charges is the ability to answer a short question, an ability the Qwen3 models already had at a high level, arriving at 29.5% and 35.5% on discursive questions, above the best trained Tucano2.

4.5By area of law

Table 4 fixes Tucano2 3.7B and opens the seven areas. The pattern of Section 4.1 holds in all of them: both pre-training regimes pay off on document writing and neither pays off on discursive questions. One note is due on a collection defect described in the datasheet: the Civil Code entered the corpus with 57 of its 2,046 articles and the ordinary Criminal Code did not enter at all, whereas the other five areas have their fundamental statute with coverage between 89% and 100%. If the value of pre-training came from reading statutory text, the gain should be smaller in those two areas. It is not: Criminal Law has the largest gain of all areas on discursive questions. What holds is the negative claim, and not an assertion about the mechanism.

4.6A coverage correction reverses the formal ordering

After the 180-condition grid was frozen, an audit of the corpus found that the statutory coverage of the card arm was far from complete. The Civil Code entered with 57 of its 2,046 articles, the ordinary Criminal Code did not enter at all, and only 6 of the 80 reference statutes reached the intended threshold; the deficit falls on exactly the material a card is made of, a provision rendered as an excerpt with questions and answers. The card arm of the frozen grid was, in effect, starved of normative text. We rebuilt both corpora to carry all 80 statutes at 98% coverage or better, changing nothing else in the budget rule, and re-ran the pipeline on Tucano2 3.7B for blocks I and VI; this is the v3 corpus. We also raised the generation cap from 1,500 to 3,000 tokens, which truncates fewer documents at the closing. To keep this section free of that second change, every number in Table 5 is measured at the 3,000-token cap, including the frozen grid, so only coverage varies down its first two rows.

Table 5 reads the raw-text/card contrast on block-I document writing. On the frozen grid the cards lose the formal part by 4.6 points and merit by 2.6, the direction reported in Section 4.2. With complete coverage the sign reverses on both cuts: the cards gain 2.4 points on the formal part and 2.2 on merit. The ordering of Section 4.2, in which raw text owns document form, is therefore specific to a corpus whose card arm lacked most of its statutory material; it does not survive completing the coverage. On block VI, where fine-tuning is largest, the formal gain narrows to 0.4 points, consistent with Section 4.1: more supervision washes out the pre-training difference.

The reversal could still be an effect of token volume or of composition, since the two corpus arms differ in both. The matched pair isolates the format. It holds the token budget at 22.35 M and the group composition identical across the two arms — case law 46.1%, statute 24.8%, case-law summaries 22.2% and enunciations 6.9% — so the only difference left is whether the material is rendered as raw text or as a card. Over ten training seeds the cards still gain 2.5 points on the formal part, paired over the seeds with t = 4.1 on nine degrees of freedom and p ≈ 0.003, with nine of the ten seeds of the same sign. On merit the gain falls to 0.9 points and is no longer distinguishable from zero, and on discursive questions there is a small cost of 0.6 points. The card format, then, carries a genuine advantage on document form once the material it is built from is present — one the frozen grid hid because its cards were missing the statutes. We report this as a correction and leave the full re-run of the grid on the completed corpus, across all four models and six blocks, to future work.
